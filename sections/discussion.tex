\toolname combines genetic variation and allele-specific methylation in a shared phasing graph with selective withholding of phase assignments predicted to be unreliable. This approach extends methods that use methylation or SVs to connect blocks in regions with few heterozygous SNVs\cite{akbari2021nanomethphase,fu2024methphaser,akbari2023parentoforigin,lin2022longphase,holt2024hiphase}. On the same reads, \toolname had 1.5--1.8-fold lower switch error rates than MethPhaser, which post-processes an SNV phasing (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}d and Supplementary Table~4). This comparison also includes the effect of GNN correction, which was not applied to the MethPhaser input. MethPhaser produced longer blocks, but these blocks contained more SNVs on the wrong haplotype (Supplementary Fig.~1e). SVs placed in the same graph were phased with 1.3--2.3-fold lower error rates than by \whatshap. On HiFi data, \toolname and HiPhase had similar SV phasing error rates overall; \toolname had lower rates for SVs of at least 1~kb and HiPhase for shorter SVs. The variant classes contributed differently, and the number of added phased sites did not predict the number of blocks joined. 5mC added about twice as many phased sites as indels but joined fewer blocks, with little change in SNV switch error rates. Indels joined most of the blocks and were associated with most of the additional switch errors. The gains from indels and 5mC were nearly additive. SVs barely changed block length, consistent with a design in which SVs never connect two blocks on their own. The same trade-off in \whatshap suggests that difficulties in indel representation or modelling may affect both methods. In joint SNV and indel phasing, \toolname with GNN correction had 8--32-fold lower switch error rates than \whatshap at variant pairs containing an indel. GNN correction reduced the additional error associated with indels (Supplementary Note~1 and Supplementary Fig.~9). More accurate indel calls could reduce the increase in phasing error associated with indel co-phasing\cite{zheng2026accelerated,kolesnikov2024local}.

Selective withholding requires a distinction between evidence agreement and confidence in phase. \toolname reports phasing entropy and weighted vote totals. Unlike model-based confidence estimates from \hapcut\cite{edge2017hapcut2} and statistical phasers\cite{hofmeister2023shapeit5}, low entropy indicates agreement among available votes, not necessarily strong evidence or a low error probability. The GNN uses neighbouring graph and sequence features and may detect candidates missed by an entropy threshold alone. However, its added value over simpler withholding rules and \hapcut's confidence pruning remains untested at matched variant retention. Its onset-prediction score is neither calibrated nor included in the output. These distinctions limit the use of the reported scores as quantitative measures of error risk.

Withholding variants and splitting blocks also change which variant pairs can be assessed. Switch error rates must therefore be considered together with completeness and contiguity. Before GNN correction, \toolname already had 1.9--2.5-fold lower switch error rates than \whatshap at similar block N50 (Supplementary Note~1). On nanopore data, \toolname phased 29--48\% fewer genotype errors than \whatshap, while it phased 0.7--1.0 percentage points fewer benchmark heterozygous SNVs; on HiFi data, the difference in completeness was 0.2--0.5 percentage points. The calls that \toolname left unphased rarely matched the benchmark: 9.8--13.0\% matched a benchmark heterozygous SNV, compared with 89.9--93.3\% of the calls that it phased. Only 3.3--7.6\% of the unphased calls were confirmed genotype errors. Most were absent from the benchmark, which identifies a false positive inside the benchmark regions but leaves a call unclassified outside them. Among the assessable benchmark heterozygous SNVs that \toolname left unphased, \whatshap phased 10--23\% incorrectly, three to six times its overall rate. SVs showed the same pattern on both platforms: given the same SNVs and SVs, \whatshap phased 0.7--6.3\% more SVs correctly than \toolname, but 1.5--2.3 times as many incorrectly (Supplementary Table~5). At 60$\times$, most SNVs in a subset left unphased at high phasing uncertainty lay outside one-to-one assembly alignments, where our analysis cannot establish whether their phase was correct.

Benchmark choice changed the apparent separation between methods. The separation in switch error rates among tools was greater against v5.0q than against GIAB v4.2.1. Many apparent errors under v4.2.1 occurred at the same variant pairs across tools, and switch-error counts in shared benchmark regions were substantially lower when the same outputs were scored against v5.0q. The counts were lower for all three phasers, including \whatshap and \hapcut, which have no parameters trained on either truth set. Errors in v4.2.1 phase transfer are one possible explanation, but shared reads, alignments and variant calls could also contribute to correlated outputs. Independent checks with reads or parental data are needed to distinguish these explanations.

Within v5.0q benchmark regions, \toolname had 2.1- to 9.5-fold lower switch error rates than \whatshap and \hapcut, including in newly covered regions. However, much of the genome-wide difference arose outside the benchmark regions of both truth sets, where the phase is least certain. This component remains exploratory. The v5.0q release evaluated variant calls rather than phase\cite{olson2026giabv5}. Our results support comparing phasers against assembly-phased truth sets, with phase errors reported separately inside and outside benchmark regions and stratified by genomic context\cite{dwarshuis2024stratifications}. Reporting which calls receive phase assignments also captures genotype errors propagated onto haplotypes, which phase-error rates alone do not measure.

Generalization remains the main evaluation limit. All data came from HG002. The network was trained on nanopore runs from the same down-sampling series, using labels from the evaluation benchmark. Genome-wide metrics include 17 training chromosomes. On held-out chromosomes, the advantage over \whatshap and \hapcut persisted, but correction removed fewer errors at a higher cost in withheld SNVs. These chromosomes were excluded from GNN training but contributed to earlier tuning of the graph constants. The HiFi results without retraining support transfer between platforms within HG002, but do not establish transfer to other individuals. Evaluation in other individuals against an independent benchmark, with fixed weights and thresholds, is needed. Subsampling variability was not estimated above 20$\times$ for nanopore data or at any HiFi coverage.

Further work should test both the scope of co-phasing and its downstream effects. 5mC evaluation measured phased fractions rather than phasing accuracy, and neither SVs nor 5mC sites had supervised training labels. SV phase was scored with one SV caller against variants called from the assembly rather than a curated SV benchmark. Methylation co-phasing was assessed only on autosomes of a male lymphoblastoid cell line\cite{fu2024methphaser}; the method assumes diploidy and balanced allele fractions. Compatibility with pipelines that use \longphase\cite{zheng2022clair3,zheng2026clairs,keskus2025severus,ont2025wfhuman} enables tests of selective withholding in haplotype-aware somatic calling. More accurate germline phase could improve tumour-read assignment, but withholding phase may reduce information available to the caller. Varying the germline phaser while fixing the somatic caller would quantify this trade-off. A calibrated per-variant confidence measure could further allow downstream methods to account explicitly for uncertainty in inherited phase.
