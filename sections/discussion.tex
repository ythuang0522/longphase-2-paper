\toolname integrates genetic variation and allele-specific methylation in a shared phasing graph, then withholds phase assignments predicted to be unreliable. Joint use of these evidence classes extends earlier approaches that use methylation or SVs to connect haplotype blocks in regions with few heterozygous SNVs\cite{akbari2021nanomethphase,fu2024methphaser,akbari2023parentoforigin,lin2022longphase,holt2024hiphase}. Whereas LongHap uses methylation to bridge blocks after sequence phasing\cite{pfennig2026longhap}, \toolname incorporates methylation and sequence evidence during haplotype construction. The evidence classes made distinct contributions: methylation increased contiguity at similar SNV switch error rates, whereas indels accounted for most of the increase in both contiguity and error. The corresponding trade-off in \whatshap suggests that this limitation is not specific to \toolname, although shared difficulties in representing or modelling indels may contribute. The indels themselves were phased with 8--31-fold fewer switch errors than by \whatshap. GNN correction reduced the additional error associated with indels, and improvements in indel calling could further improve this balance\cite{zheng2026accelerated,kolesnikov2024local}.

Selective withholding complements evidence integration by reducing reliance on uncertain phase assignments. \toolname reports phasing entropy and weighted vote totals as measures of the underlying evidence. These differ from the model-based confidence estimates provided by \hapcut\cite{edge2017hapcut2} and statistical phasers\cite{hofmeister2023shapeit5}: when votes are present, low entropy indicates their agreement, not necessarily strong evidence or a low probability of error. The GNN incorporates neighbouring graph and sequence features, which may allow it to identify candidates that a high-entropy threshold alone would miss. However, its added value over simpler withholding rules and \hapcut's confidence pruning remains to be established at matched variant retention. Its phase-error score is neither calibrated nor included in the output. Because withholding variants and splitting blocks change which variant pairs can be assessed, reductions in switch error rate must be interpreted jointly with phased fraction and contiguity. Margin's lower error rate but shorter blocks at 10$\times$ genome-wide, and at 10--12$\times$ on the held-out chromosomes, illustrate this trade-off. Most SNVs unphased by the GNN lay outside regions with one-to-one assembly alignments, where the present analysis cannot establish whether their phase was correct.

Benchmark choice substantially altered the measured separation between methods. At 30--60$\times$, the highest and lowest switch error rates differed by 1.2--1.3-fold against GIAB v4.2.1 but by 4.3--4.8-fold against v5.0q. Two effects contributed. Against v4.2.1 at high coverage, most switch errors of the three tools fell at the same variant pairs. In regions that both benchmarks assess, the same phased outputs had 1,127--1,492 switch errors against v4.2.1 but 32--393 against v5.0q at 30--60$\times$, which indicates that the two truth sets differ in phase there. All three phasers agreed with v5.0q at most of these sites, including \whatshap and \hapcut, which use no parameters trained on either truth set. Errors in the v4.2.1 phase transfer are one possible explanation. The phasers share reads, alignments and variant calls, however, and we did not check these sites independently, for example in the reads or with parental data. Within the v5.0q benchmark regions, \toolname made 2.1- to 10-fold fewer switch errors than the other phasers. Most of the remaining genome-wide separation lay at assembly-phased variants outside the benchmark regions of both truth sets, where the truth phase is least certain; this part of the comparison is exploratory, and scoring outside the benchmark regions is a limitation of our evaluation. The v5.0q release evaluated variant calls but not phase\cite{olson2026giabv5}. Our results indicate that phasers should be compared against assembly-phased truth sets, with errors reported inside and outside the benchmark regions and stratified by genomic context\cite{dwarshuis2024stratifications}.

The current evaluation has several limits. All data derive from HG002, and the network was trained on nanopore runs from the same down-sampling series, using labels from the evaluation benchmark. Genome-wide metrics include the 17 chromosomes that contributed training windows. On the three held-out chromosomes, the advantage over \whatshap and \hapcut was unchanged, but GNN correction removed fewer switch errors (14--23\% versus 31--33\% genome-wide) at a higher cost per removed error (10.5 versus 8.3 withheld SNVs), and these results measure performance on chromosomes excluded from network training. These chromosomes were held out from network training but not from the earlier tuning of the graph constants, and they come from the same individual. Evaluation in other individuals against an independent benchmark, with weights and thresholds fixed in advance, is still needed. The HiFi results without retraining are consistent with transfer between sequencing platforms within HG002, but do not establish generalization to other individuals. Subsampling variability was not estimated for nanopore coverages above 20$\times$ or for HiFi. For SVs and 5mC, we measured phased fractions rather than phasing accuracy, and these sites had no supervised training labels. Methylation co-phasing was assessed only on autosomes of a male lymphoblastoid cell line\cite{fu2024methphaser}. The method also assumes a diploid genome with balanced allele fractions.

Compatibility with pipelines that use \longphase\cite{zheng2022clair3,zheng2026clairs,keskus2025severus,ont2025wfhuman} enables evaluation of the downstream effects of selective phase withholding. In haplotype-aware somatic calling\cite{zheng2026clairs,keskus2025severus}, reducing errors in germline phase could improve the assignment of tumour reads to haplotypes, but withholding phase may also reduce the information available to the caller. Varying the germline phaser while keeping the somatic caller fixed would quantify this trade-off. A calibrated per-variant confidence measure would further allow downstream methods to account explicitly for uncertainty in inherited phase.
