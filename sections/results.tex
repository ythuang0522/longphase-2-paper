\subsection*{\toolname combines joint phasing with learned error detection}
\toolname builds one phasing graph per chromosome from SNVs, small indels, SVs and allele-specific 5mC sites called by its \texttt{modcall} module (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a). Read observations are weighted by base quality and sequence context, and filters reduce the influence of observations in homopolymers, tandem repeats and candidate copy-number-altered regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). Every phased variant then receives a phasing-uncertainty score, and a graph neural network (GNN) examines windows centred on high-uncertainty sites. Variants that the GNN predicts to be misphased are unphased, with their genotypes retained, and blocks disconnected by the removal are split (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c); we refer to this step as GNN correction. The output comprises phased VCFs for each variant class and haplotagged alignments (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d).

We evaluated \toolname on HG002 nanopore R10.4.1 reads down-sampled to 10--60$\times$ and on PacBio HiFi reads from the same individual at 10--50$\times$ (Methods). Nanopore data were sampled in 2$\times$ steps from 10 to 20$\times$, with ten replicates per coverage, and once each at 30, 40, 50 and 60$\times$; HiFi data and the analyses of unphased variants used one down-sampling per coverage. All phasers received the same alignments and variant calls. Phasing was scored against two HG002 truth sets, each used in full, including variants outside its benchmark regions: GIAB v4.2.1, integrated from mapping-based call sets, and GIAB v5.0q, in which every heterozygous variant is phased by the diploid telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assembly (Methods). Unless stated otherwise, results refer to SNV phasing against v5.0q, and \toolname denotes the complete method, including GNN correction. Per-run values are provided in Supplementary Data~1 and summaries in Supplementary Tables~7--16.

\subsection*{Comparison with existing phasers}

\paragraph{\toolname has the lowest SNV switch error rate at every coverage.}
In SNV-only phasing, \toolname had a lower switch error rate than \whatshap and \hapcut at each of the ten coverages, decreasing from 0.082\% at 10$\times$ to 0.023\% at 60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}a). The separation widened between 10 and 20$\times$ and then remained stable: the rate of \toolname was 2.8- and 3.3-fold lower than those of \whatshap and \hapcut at 10$\times$, and 3.4--3.7- and 4.3--4.8-fold lower at 20--60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~8). The difference held in every down-sampling replicate: compared within the same replicate at 10--20$\times$, the rate of \toolname was 2.8--4.1-fold lower than that of \whatshap and 3.2--5.1-fold lower than that of \hapcut. \toolname also had the lowest block-wise Hamming distance at every coverage, 1.3--1.8-fold below those of the other tools (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}b). Block N50 increased with coverage at a similar rate for the three tools, from 0.84--0.95~Mb at 10$\times$ to 2.72--2.86~Mb at 60$\times$; that of \toolname was up to 11\% shorter than that of \whatshap below 30$\times$ and 1--5\% longer from 30$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}c). The phased fraction of benchmark heterozygous SNVs rose steeply up to 16$\times$ and levelled off at 91--92\% from 20$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}d); at every coverage, \whatshap and \hapcut phased 0.7--1.0 percentage points more of these SNVs than \toolname.

The advantage did not depend on the chromosomes used to train the GNN. On chr17, chr21 and chr22, which were held out from training, every tool had higher switch error rates than genome-wide, and \toolname had 2.8--3.9-fold lower rates than \whatshap and 3.3--4.9-fold lower rates than \hapcut at 10--20$\times$ (2.7--4.9-fold at 30--60$\times$, from single runs with 37--83 switch errors by \toolname; Supplementary Table~15).

Of two further phasers run at 10--20$\times$ (Supplementary Fig.~10), Ralphi\cite{battistella2025ralphi} had 2.5--3.5-fold higher switch error rates than \toolname. Margin\cite{shafin2021margin} had the lowest rate of all tools at 10$\times$ (0.069\% versus 0.082\%) and the same rate as \toolname at 12$\times$, but its rate decreased little with coverage and was 1.2--1.5-fold higher than that of \toolname at 14--20$\times$; its blocks were 2.1--3.2-fold shorter at every coverage. The held-out chromosomes showed the same trade-off: Margin had lower rates than \toolname at 10 and 12$\times$ (0.099\% versus 0.127\% at 10$\times$), with 3.3-fold shorter blocks (Supplementary Table~15). GCphase was not evaluated because it treats every input record as a heterozygous SNV (Methods).

\toolname was also faster (Supplementary Table~16). On one thread, SNV phasing with GNN correction took 8.1~min at 10$\times$ and 18.0~min at 60$\times$, compared with 25.2 and 113.8~min for \whatshap and 24.1 and 96.4~min for \hapcut; between these coverages, its runtime increased 2.2-fold, compared with 4.0--4.5-fold for the other tools. At 60$\times$, its peak memory was 7.4~GiB on one thread and 63.0~GiB with 24 threads (16.3~min), compared with 4.0~GiB for \whatshap and 19.4~GiB for \hapcut.

\paragraph{The advantage of \toolname extends to indels and increases with coverage.}
\whatshap can also phase indels with SNVs; doing so lengthened its blocks by 31--59\% but raised its SNV switch error rate by 17--20\% at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}a,c,e,g). With the same indels, \toolname kept 3.0--3.7-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}e, solid lines), and the difference was larger for the indels themselves (dotted lines). For consecutive variant pairs that included an indel, the switch error rate of \toolname decreased with coverage from 0.101\% to 0.030\%, 8--25\% above its SNV rate, whereas that of \whatshap remained at 0.75--0.91\%, three- to ninefold above its SNV rate. The difference therefore grew with coverage, from 8-fold at 10$\times$ to 31-fold at 60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~14). The indel Hamming distance followed the same pattern, decreasing from 6.8\% to 3.5\% for \toolname but remaining at 9.0--13.8\% for \whatshap (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}f). In exchange, \whatshap phased 2.4--4.5 percentage points more benchmark heterozygous indels, a difference that increased with coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}h), and built longer blocks, although this difference narrowed from 28--34\% at 10--18$\times$ to 14\% at 60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}g).

\paragraph{Joint SNV and 5mC phasing makes fewer switch errors than MethPhaser.}
MethPhaser\cite{fu2024methphaser} adds methylation evidence to existing SNV phase blocks; we gave it the SNV-only output of \toolname without GNN correction (Methods). Joint SNV and 5mC phasing by \toolname had 1.4--1.5-fold lower switch error rates than MethPhaser at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}i) and lower Hamming distances, although this difference decreased from 1.6 percentage points at 10$\times$ to at most 0.2 at 50--60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}j). Its block N50 was 6\% shorter at 10$\times$ and 3--11\% longer at 20--60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}k), and the phased fractions agreed to within 0.2 percentage points (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}l). MethPhaser returned its input blocks with unchanged switch errors and block N50 at every coverage. Before GNN correction, joint phasing made as many switch errors as MethPhaser (within 2\%) and produced 2--13\% longer blocks (Supplementary Fig.~11). The lower switch error rates in Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}i are therefore attributable to GNN correction, and the longer blocks to the use of 5mC during phasing.

\paragraph{Indels account for most of the gain in contiguity and of the increase in switch errors.}
Within \toolname, each added evidence class lengthened blocks at every coverage, but the classes differed in their effect on accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}a,b). Adding 5mC kept the SNV switch error rate within 4\% of that of SNV-only phasing and increased block N50 by 2\% at 10$\times$ and by 8--14\% from 18$\times$. Adding indels increased block N50 by 15--32\% but also raised the switch error rate by 12--21\%. Phasing all four classes gave the longest blocks at every coverage, 19--47\% longer than those of SNV-only phasing (4.10 versus 2.86~Mb at 60$\times$), at 14--27\% higher switch error rates; adding SVs and 5mC to indels changed the switch error rate at indel pairs by at most 6\% (Supplementary Table~14). When the two metrics are plotted against each other (Fig.~\ref{fig:evidence}c), every configuration of \toolname has a lower switch error rate than both configurations of \whatshap at the same coverage. Four-class phasing approached the block N50 of \whatshap with indels as coverage increased, from 19--21\% shorter at 10--14$\times$ to 2--3\% shorter at 50--60$\times$, at 3.0--3.7-fold lower switch error rates (0.027\% versus 0.097\% at 60$\times$).

\paragraph{Benchmark choice changes the measured separation between phasers.}
Against GIAB v4.2.1, the three phasers converged as coverage increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}a). \toolname made 1,635--1,820 switch errors at every coverage, whereas the switch errors of \whatshap and \hapcut decreased from 2,773 and 3,050 at 10$\times$ to 1,801 and 2,088 at 60$\times$, so the ratio between the highest and the lowest rate decreased from 1.7 to 1.2--1.3 at 30--60$\times$. Against v5.0q, the same phased VCFs did not converge (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}b): the switch errors of every tool decreased with coverage, those of \toolname from 1,518 to 496, and the ratio increased from 3.3 to 4.3--4.8 (Supplementary Table~9). Relative to v4.2.1, v5.0q lowered the switch errors of \toolname by 17\% at 10$\times$ and by 70\% at 60$\times$, whereas it raised those of \hapcut at every coverage and those of \whatshap below 60$\times$ (both 1.6-fold at 10$\times$). Hamming distance changed little between the truth sets.

Against v4.2.1, the tools made an increasing share of their switch errors at the same SNV pairs as coverage increased. The share of the switch errors of \toolname that coincided with switch errors of both other tools rose from 63\% at 10$\times$ to 93\% at 60$\times$, where the same set accounted for 86\% of the switch errors of \whatshap and 75\% of those of \hapcut; against v5.0q, the corresponding shares at 60$\times$ were 37\%, 10\% and 8\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}c and Supplementary Table~13). Within the regions assessed by both truth sets, each tool made 1,127--1,492 switch errors against v4.2.1 at 30--60$\times$ but 32--393 against v5.0q, so the two truth sets differ in phase at many sites there. Whether this reflects errors in the v4.2.1 phase transfer has not been examined in the reads.

Within the v5.0q benchmark regions, \toolname had the lowest switch error rate at every coverage, and the separation increased with coverage, from 2.1-fold relative to \whatshap and 2.6-fold relative to \hapcut at 10$\times$ to 3.5--4.0-fold and 8.0--10-fold at 30--60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~13). Among GIAB genomic strata\cite{dwarshuis2024stratifications}, the separation was largest in segmental duplications (4.3--6.5-fold at 10 and 60$\times$) and smallest outside difficult regions (1.7--2.0-fold; Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}d,e). As coverage increased, a growing share of the switch errors of every tool lay outside the benchmark regions of both truth sets, where the truth phase is least certain: 29\% at 10$\times$ and 70\% at 60$\times$ for \toolname, and 43--46\% and 62--75\% for the other tools. There, \toolname had switch error rates of 0.8--1.2\% and the other tools 2.6--4.9\%. These sites accounted for 50--56\% of the genome-wide difference between \toolname and the other tools at 10$\times$ and for 60--77\% at 60$\times$; we regard this part of the comparison as exploratory.

\paragraph{The advantage of \toolname carries over to PacBio HiFi data without retraining.}
On HiFi data, with the network trained on nanopore data, \toolname had 2.2--2.6-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:hifi}a and Supplementary Table~11). Because the rate of \whatshap decreased more steeply with coverage (from 0.128\% to 0.077\% between 10 and 50$\times$, versus 0.052\% to 0.034\%), the difference narrowed from 2.5--2.6-fold at 10--30$\times$ to 2.2--2.3-fold at 40--50$\times$. Hamming distances were 0.7--1.2\% for both tools; that of \toolname was lower at 10--40$\times$ and higher at 50$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hifi}b). Block N50 increased from 340 to 580~kb and differed by at most 3\% between the tools (Fig.~\ref{fig:hifi}c), and \whatshap phased 0.4--0.5 percentage points more benchmark heterozygous SNVs (Fig.~\ref{fig:hifi}d). HiFi blocks were shorter than nanopore blocks, consistent with the shorter HiFi reads, and the gap widened from 2.5-fold at 10$\times$ to 4.3-fold at 50$\times$ because block N50 increased 1.7-fold over this range on HiFi data but 3.0-fold on nanopore data.

\paragraph{\toolname and \whatshap leave different SNVs unphased.}
Among the heterozygous SNV calls present in both outputs, \toolname left more unphased than \whatshap, but the difference decreased from 1.9-fold at 10$\times$ to 1.5-fold at 60$\times$ (210,683 versus 142,313; Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}a). As coverage increased, the calls unphased by \toolname alone decreased from 149,000 to 122,000, whereas those unphased by \whatshap alone increased from 38,000 to 54,000; 89,000--97,000 were unphased by both. Each tool-specific set was enriched in intervals that contained switch disagreements between the other tool and the benchmark, and the enrichment increased with coverage, from 4.3- to 9.6-fold for SNVs unphased by \toolname alone and from 2.0- to 9.5-fold for those unphased by \whatshap alone (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}b). These enrichments describe regional co-location, not the correctness of individual phase assignments. The read depths of the sets also diverged with coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}c and Supplementary Fig.~12). At 10$\times$, 64--71\% of the sites in each set had a depth below 20$\times$. At 30 and 60$\times$, this held for 47--60\% of the sites unphased by \whatshap alone or by both tools, but for only 12--16\% of those unphased by \toolname alone, whose depths peaked at the sequencing coverage; at 60$\times$, 15.6\% of them were at or above the depth cap of the variant caller\todo{The depth shares are computed from the 2$\times$-bin histograms in \texttt{UnphaseVenn3.jsx}, the source of Fig.~6c, which exist in no committed table; add the per-set depth summaries to Supplementary Data~1}. \whatshap thus withheld phase mainly where reads were sparse, whereas \toolname also withheld it at sites of typical or excess depth; capped depths alone do not identify the source of excess read coverage.

\subsection*{Contribution of the internal modules}

\paragraph{GNN correction removes one-third of SNV switch errors.}
Before GNN correction, \toolname already had 1.9--2.5-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and 2.2--3.3-fold lower rates than \hapcut (Supplementary Fig.~13b and Supplementary Table~8). GNN correction lowered these rates by a further 31--33\% at every coverage (30--36\% in individual replicates at 10--20$\times$), at a cost that decreased with coverage: the phased fraction fell by 0.26 percentage points at 10$\times$ and by 0.09--0.11 at 30--60$\times$, and block N50 by 5--9\% at 10--20$\times$ and 2--5\% at 30--60$\times$ (Supplementary Fig.~13a,d). On the held-out chromosomes, GNN correction removed 20--23\% of switch errors at 10--20$\times$ and 14--20\% at 30--60$\times$ (9--15 per single run), and it withheld 10.5 phased SNVs per removed switch error, compared with 8.3 over the whole genome (Supplementary Table~15). Because these chromosomes were harder to phase for every tool, the smaller reduction may reflect both sequence not seen in training and chromosome difficulty. On HiFi data, GNN correction removed 19--25\% of switch errors and reduced the phased fraction by at most 0.1 percentage points and block N50 by 0.6--1.6\% (Supplementary Fig.~13e,f).

\paragraph{GNN correction reduces the additional error of indel co-phasing.}
We evaluated seven combinations of variant classes, each with and without GNN correction (Supplementary Figs.~14 and~15 and Supplementary Table~10). Without correction, adding SVs increased block N50 by 1--2\% and adding 5mC by 2--13\%, and neither changed the SNV switch error rate by more than 3\%. Adding indels increased block N50 by 29--51\% (32--75\% together with SVs and 5mC) but also raised the SNV switch error rate by 24--34\% at every coverage. GNN correction reduced switch error rates by 30--33\% in configurations without indels and by 34--42\% in those with indels, removing 41--71\% of the additional error associated with indels and 24--39\% of the switch errors at indel pairs (Supplementary Table~14). It shortened blocks by 12--22\% in indel-containing configurations, compared with 1--9\% in the others, as unphasing disconnected blocks that indels had bridged.

\paragraph{SNVs unphased by GNN correction lie mostly outside the benchmark and outside one-to-one assembly alignments.}
GNN correction unphased 55,756--58,162 SNVs per genome at 10--60$\times$ (one replicate per coverage). Classified by position against the v5.0q benchmark VCF, the share absent from it increased with coverage from 83.0\% at 10$\times$ to 93.0\% at 60$\times$, and the share heterozygous in it decreased from 11.9\% (6,650) to 4.0\% (2,215); the remainder were homozygous (Supplementary Fig.~16a). Requiring matching REF and ALT alleles left 6,405 and 2,055 heterozygous benchmark SNVs. Absence from the benchmark VCF does not by itself establish that a call is erroneous, because the benchmark depends on assembly alignment and variant representation.

In an exploratory analysis of the T2T-HG002 assembly alignments, 11.8\% of the 56,076 SNVs unphased at 60$\times$ lay in regions where both assembled haplotypes aligned one-to-one to GRCh38, compared with 92.6\% of a random sample of SNVs phased before correction (odds ratio for lying outside these regions, 94; Supplementary Fig.~16b and Supplementary Table~12). These counts include chrX and chrY; on the autosomes alone, the shares were 9.6\% and 92.9\% (odds ratio, 124), and the enrichment was observed on every autosome (Supplementary Fig.~16c). Sites outside these regions clustered in pericentromeric satellites, particularly at chr11:51--54~Mb, chr12:35--36~Mb and chr18:15--20~Mb. Of the 6,601 unphased SNVs within one-to-one alignments, 3,421 (52\%) had no assembly variant record at the same start coordinate; because this test does not compare alleles, it can miss variants that the assembly represents at another position. The analysis has three further limits. The background sample included sites that GNN correction later unphased, which biases the comparison towards no difference. The one-to-one regions come from the raw assembly alignment, which retains assembly errors that the benchmark curation removes. Finally, because v5.0q derives from the same assembly, the analysis extends the assessment beyond the benchmark regions but does not independently validate phase or allele identity.
