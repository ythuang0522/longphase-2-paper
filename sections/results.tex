\subsection*{\toolname combines joint phasing with learned error detection}
\toolname integrates SNVs, small indels, SVs and allele-specific 5mC sites called by its \texttt{modcall} module in a chromosome-wide phasing graph (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a). Observations are weighted by base quality and sequence context, with filters for homopolymers, tandem repeats and candidate copy-number-altered regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). A graph neural network (GNN) examines windows centred on variants with high phasing uncertainty. The GNN predicts phase-disagreement onsets within these windows. GNN correction selectively removes phase assignments at the predicted sites, preserves variant genotypes and splits blocks disconnected by unphasing (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c). The outputs are phased VCFs for each variant class and haplotagged alignments (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d).

We evaluated \toolname on HG002 nanopore R10.4.1 reads at 10--60$\times$ and PacBio HiFi reads from the same individual at 10--50$\times$ (Methods). Nanopore data comprised ten down-sampling replicates per coverage at 10--20$\times$ in 2$\times$ steps, and one each at 30, 40, 50 and 60$\times$; HiFi and unphased-variant analyses used one replicate per coverage. All phasers received the same alignments and variant calls. We scored phase against two HG002 truth sets: GIAB v4.2.1, integrated from mapping-based call sets, and GIAB v5.0q, phased by the diploid telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assembly. Both truth sets were used in full, including variants outside their benchmark regions. SV phase was scored against the variants of the T2T-HG002 assembly, which resolves both alleles of every SV (Methods). Unless stated otherwise, results refer to SNV phasing against v5.0q and include GNN correction. Per-run values and summaries are in Supplementary Data~1 and Supplementary Tables~7--18.

\subsection*{\toolname reduces switch error rates across evidence types and sequencing platforms}
In SNV-only phasing of nanopore data, \toolname consistently achieved lower switch error rates (0.022--0.081\%) than \whatshap (0.081--0.233\%) and \hapcut (0.104--0.266\%) across the tested coverages (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}a). These rates corresponded to 2.9--3.9-fold and 3.3--5.0-fold reductions relative to \whatshap and \hapcut, respectively (Supplementary Table~8). \toolname also had 1.3--1.8-fold lower block-wise Hamming distances, the fraction of misassigned variants within blocks, than \whatshap and \hapcut (Supplementary Fig.~16a). When indels were included in the nanopore runs, \toolname had 3.0--3.7-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and 3.5--4.8-fold lower rates than \hapcut (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}b, solid lines). The difference was larger for consecutive variant pairs containing an indel: rates decreased from 0.100\% to 0.029\% for \toolname but remained at 0.75--0.91\% for \whatshap (dotted lines). The reduction increased from 8-fold at 10$\times$ to 32-fold at 60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~14). Indel Hamming distances showed the same pattern, decreasing from 6.9\% to 3.6\% for \toolname and remaining at 9.0--13.8\% for \whatshap (Supplementary Fig.~16b).

\subsection*{\toolname phases SVs with lower error rates than \whatshap}
We scored the phase of heterozygous deletions and insertions of at least 50~bp called by Sniffles2; every tool received the same SNV and SV calls. On nanopore data, 5.8--11.3\% of the SVs phased by \toolname had the wrong phase at 10--60$\times$, compared with 11.2--18.7\% for \whatshap, 1.6--2.1-fold lower at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}a and Supplementary Table~20). \whatshap phased 0.7--6.3\% more SVs correctly, but 1.8--2.3 times as many incorrectly, and 2.7--3.3 times as many SVs that are homozygous in the assembly. On HiFi data, the SV error rates of \toolname were 5.8--6.6\%, 1.4--1.5-fold lower than those of \whatshap (8.9--9.4\%) at every coverage and similar to those of HiPhase (5.4--6.4\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}b); HiPhase phased 7--9\% more SVs correctly.

The difference depended on SV size (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}c). For SVs of at least 1~kb, \toolname had the lowest error rate at every coverage on both platforms: 8.0--12.8\% on nanopore data, compared with 15.2--20.7\% for \whatshap, and 7.8--9.1\% on HiFi data, compared with 10.9--13.3\% for HiPhase and 13.1--14.3\% for \whatshap. For SVs of 50~bp to 1~kb on HiFi data, HiPhase had the lowest error rates (4.8--5.8\%, compared with 5.6--6.5\% for \toolname; Supplementary Table~20). Of the deletions of at least 10~kb, across which aligners often split reads into separate alignments, \toolname phased 32--38 correctly on HiFi data with at most three errors, compared with 21--28 for HiPhase. With indels co-phased, \toolname had SV error rates of 7.2--12.1\% and 7.1--7.8\% on nanopore and HiFi data, compared with 16.3--22.1\% and 9.9--12.4\% for \whatshap, and phased 1--9\% more SVs correctly than \whatshap on both platforms. HiPhase had higher error rates than \toolname at 10--30$\times$ and lower at 40 and 50$\times$ (6.9--8.6\%), and phased 16--19\% more SVs correctly (Supplementary Fig.~17).

\subsection*{Joint 5mC phasing gives lower error rates than methylation post-processing}
On nanopore data, joint SNV and 5mC phasing by \toolname had the lowest switch error rate of the four phasers that use 5mC at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}d and Supplementary Table~18). It had 1.5--1.8-fold lower switch error rates and 1.4--3.1-fold lower Hamming distances than MethPhaser\cite{fu2024methphaser} (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}d and Supplementary Fig.~16e). MethPhaser produced longer blocks, with N50 1.1-fold that of \toolname at 10$\times$ and 1.3--1.8-fold at 20--60$\times$ (0.96--5.49 versus 0.87--3.12~Mb; Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}e), whereas phased fractions differed by approximately 0.2 percentage points or less (Supplementary Table~18). GNN correction accounted for 66--95\% of the switch-error difference; most of the remainder came from the block joins of MethPhaser, which increased switch errors in its input by 2--15\% (Supplementary Fig.~11). HapBridge\cite{chen2025hapbridge}, given the same input as MethPhaser, had 1.5--1.6-fold higher switch error rates and 1.2--1.5-fold higher Hamming distances than \toolname at every coverage, with block N50 0.95--1.34-fold that of \toolname; it left its input unchanged at 10--20$\times$ and joined blocks at 30--60$\times$ while changing the number of switch errors by about 1\% or less.

\subsection*{Benchmark choice changes the measured separation between phasers}
Against GIAB v4.2.1, differences in switch error rates among \toolname, \whatshap and \hapcut narrowed as coverage increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}a). \toolname made 1,641--1,839 switch errors across coverages, whereas \whatshap and \hapcut decreased from 2,773 and 3,050 at 10$\times$ to 1,801 and 2,088 at 60$\times$. The ratio between the highest and lowest switch error rates fell from 1.6 to 1.2--1.3 at 30--60$\times$. When assessed against v5.0q, switch-error counts decreased with increasing coverage for all three tools, including from 1,503 to 477 for \toolname, and the rate ratio increased from 3.3 to 4.3--5.0 (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}b). Relative to v4.2.1, v5.0q reduced errors for \toolname but increased them for \hapcut at every coverage and for \whatshap below 60$\times$. Hamming distance changed little (Supplementary Table~9).

Coincident switch errors accounted for much of the convergence under v4.2.1. The fraction of \toolname switch errors shared by both other tools increased from 63\% at 10$\times$ to 93\% at 60$\times$. At 60$\times$, these errors accounted for 86\% of \whatshap errors and 74\% of \hapcut errors; corresponding shares under v5.0q were only 38\%, 10\% and 8\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}c). In shared benchmark regions at 30--60$\times$, each tool made 1,133--1,492 switch errors against v4.2.1 but 32--393 against v5.0q (Supplementary Table~13). The truth sets thus differ in phase at many shared sites, although possible v4.2.1 phase-transfer errors have not been checked in the reads.

The separation between phasers also depended on genomic context. Within the v5.0q benchmark regions, \toolname had the lowest switch error rate of the three tools at every coverage. Rates were 2.1-fold lower than for \whatshap and 2.6-fold lower than for \hapcut at 10$\times$, increasing to 3.3--3.8-fold and 7.8--9.5-fold lower at 30--60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~13). Among GIAB genomic strata\cite{dwarshuis2024stratifications}, the separation was largest in segmental duplications (3.2--5.4-fold at 10 and 60$\times$) and smallest outside difficult regions (1.7--2.2-fold; Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}d,e). The relative advantage of \toolname was greater in regions covered by v5.0q but not v4.2.1\cite{olson2026giabv5}: \whatshap and \hapcut had 4.2--8.3 and 7.1--11 times its switch error rate, compared with 1.7--2.5 and 2.0--9.4 times in regions covered by both benchmarks (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}d,e). These comparisons involved 18--41 switch errors for \toolname in the newly covered regions per coverage.

Outside the benchmark regions of both truth sets, where the truth phase is least certain, switch errors accounted for an increasing share of each tool's total as coverage rose. This share increased from 28\% to 69\% for \toolname and from 43--46\% to 62--75\% for the other tools between 10 and 60$\times$. Rates in these regions were 0.8--1.1\% for \toolname and 2.6--4.9\% for the other tools. These sites contributed 50--56\% of the genome-wide difference in switch-error counts at 10$\times$ and 60--77\% at 60$\times$. We therefore treat this component of the comparison as exploratory (Supplementary Table~13).

An exploratory analysis of T2T-HG002 assembly alignments placed 11.4\% of the 62,230 SNVs unphased at 60$\times$ in regions where both haplotypes aligned one-to-one to GRCh38. This compared with 92.6\% of a random sample phased before correction (odds ratio for lying outside these regions, 98; Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}g and Supplementary Table~12). This assembly comparison identifies genomic context but does not independently validate phase or allele identity. Coordinate matching can miss variants represented at another position. The background sample included sites later unphased by correction, which biases the comparison towards no difference. The one-to-one regions came from raw assembly alignments and retain assembly errors removed during benchmark curation. Moreover, v5.0q derives from the same assembly. Thus, the analysis extends the assessment beyond benchmark regions, but cannot establish whether the excluded phase assignments were correct.

\subsection*{GNN correction reduces genome-wide SNV switch error rates by one-third and limits indel-associated errors}
Without GNN correction, \toolname had 1.9--2.5-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and 2.2--3.3-fold lower rates than \hapcut (Supplementary Fig.~13b and Supplementary Table~8). GNN correction reduced these rates by a further 32--36\% at every coverage (31--38\% across individual replicates at 10--20$\times$). The associated loss of completeness decreased with coverage, from 0.26 percentage points at 10$\times$ to 0.08--0.11 at 30--60$\times$. Block N50 decreased by 5--9\% at 10--20$\times$ and 1--4\% at 30--60$\times$ (Supplementary Fig.~13a,d).

The correction effect was smaller on chromosomes excluded from GNN training (chr17, chr21 and chr22) and on HiFi data. On held-out chromosomes, correction removed 22--25\% of switch errors at 10--20$\times$ and 14--20\% at 30--60$\times$ (9--17 per single run). It withheld 9.3 phased SNVs per removed error, compared with 8.0 genome-wide (Supplementary Table~15). Every tool had higher switch error rates on these chromosomes than genome-wide. The smaller reduction may therefore reflect both chromosome difficulty and sequence absent from training. After correction, \toolname still had 2.7--5.1-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and \hapcut on these chromosomes across the tested coverages (Supplementary Table~15). On HiFi data, the nanopore-trained network removed 23--31\% of switch errors without retraining, while reducing completeness by at most 0.12 percentage points and block N50 by 0.8--1.6\% (Supplementary Fig.~13e,f).
