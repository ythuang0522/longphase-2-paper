\subsection*{\toolname combines joint phasing with learned error detection}
\toolname integrates SNVs, small indels, SVs and allele-specific 5mC sites called by its \texttt{modcall} module in a chromosome-wide phasing graph (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a). Observations are weighted by base quality and sequence context, with filters for homopolymers, tandem repeats and candidate copy-number-altered regions (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). A graph neural network (GNN) examines windows centred on variants with high phasing uncertainty. The GNN predicts phase-disagreement onsets. Variants at these predicted onsets are then selectively unphased, their genotypes are retained, and blocks disconnected by unphasing are split (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c). We call this step GNN correction. The outputs are phased VCFs for each variant class and haplotagged alignments (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d).

We evaluated \toolname on HG002 nanopore R10.4.1 reads at 10--60$\times$ and PacBio HiFi reads from the same individual at 10--50$\times$ (Methods). Nanopore data comprised ten down-sampling replicates per coverage at 10--20$\times$ in 2$\times$ steps, and one each at 30, 40, 50 and 60$\times$; HiFi and unphased-variant analyses used one replicate per coverage. All phasers received the same alignments and variant calls. We scored phase against two HG002 truth sets: GIAB v4.2.1, integrated from mapping-based call sets, and GIAB v5.0q, phased by the diploid telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assembly. Both truth sets were used in full, including variants outside their benchmark regions. Unless stated otherwise, results refer to SNV phasing against v5.0q and include GNN correction. Per-run values and summaries are in Supplementary Data~1 and Supplementary Tables~7--17.

\subsection*{\toolname reduces switch error rates across evidence types and sequencing platforms}
In SNV-only phasing, \toolname had lower switch error rates than \whatshap and \hapcut at all ten coverages, decreasing from 0.081\% at 10$\times$ to 0.022\% at 60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}a). The separation widened up to 20$\times$ and then remained stable. Rates were 2.9- and 3.3-fold lower than those of \whatshap and \hapcut at 10$\times$, respectively, and 3.4--3.9- and 4.3--5.0-fold lower at 20--60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~8). This difference held in every paired replicate at 10--20$\times$ (2.8--4.2-fold versus \whatshap; 3.2--5.2-fold versus \hapcut). \toolname also had 1.3--1.8-fold lower block-wise Hamming distances than these tools at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}b). The separation in switch error rates was largest in regions newly covered by the v5.0q benchmark (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}d,e).

Lower switch error rates were accompanied by similar block contiguity (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}c). Block N50 increased from 0.84--0.95~Mb at 10$\times$ to 2.72--2.87~Mb at 60$\times$ across the three tools. The blocks of \toolname were up to 11\% shorter than those of \whatshap at 10--20$\times$ and 2--5\% longer at 30--60$\times$. The low-coverage shortfall arose from block splitting during GNN correction. Before correction, \toolname already had 1.9--2.5-fold lower switch error rates than \whatshap, with block N50 between 3\% shorter and 7\% longer at every coverage (Supplementary Table~8). Co-phasing indels, SVs and 5mC with SNVs extended the blocks of \toolname to 6--52\% beyond those of \whatshap in SNV-only mode, while retaining 2.5--3.2-fold lower SNV switch error rates (Supplementary Tables~8 and~10). We assess the accompanying differences in phased fraction below (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}).

The reduction in indel-pair switch error rates relative to \whatshap increased with coverage. Adding indels lengthened the blocks of \whatshap by 31--59\% but increased its SNV switch error rate by 17--20\% at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}c,f). With the same indels, \toolname had 3.0--3.7-fold lower SNV switch error rates (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}d, solid lines). The difference was larger for consecutive variant pairs containing an indel: rates decreased from 0.100\% to 0.029\% for \toolname, but remained at 0.75--0.91\% for \whatshap (dotted lines). The reduction relative to \whatshap therefore increased from 8-fold at 10$\times$ to 32-fold at 60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~14). Indel Hamming distance also decreased for \toolname, from 6.9\% to 3.6\%, but remained at 9.0--13.8\% for \whatshap (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}e).

With SNVs and indels, \whatshap produced longer blocks than \toolname, but the difference narrowed with coverage: its blocks were 27--33\% longer at 10--18$\times$ and 12\% longer at 60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}f). Adding SVs and 5mC, which \whatshap cannot use, brought the blocks of \toolname closer to those of \whatshap with indels: they were 1--20\% shorter across coverages and 1\% shorter at 50--60$\times$. SNV switch error rates remained 3.0--3.8-fold lower (0.026\% versus 0.097\% at 60$\times$; dashed line).

Joint SNV and 5mC phasing by \toolname had 1.5--1.7-fold lower switch error rates than MethPhaser at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}g). MethPhaser\cite{fu2024methphaser} adds methylation evidence to existing SNV phase blocks; we supplied the SNV-only output of \toolname before GNN correction (Methods). The Hamming distance of \toolname was 1.3-fold lower at 10$\times$ and 2.1--2.9-fold lower at 20--60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}h). MethPhaser produced longer blocks, with N50 1.1-fold that of \toolname at 10$\times$ and 1.3--1.8-fold at 20--60$\times$ (0.96--5.49 versus 0.88--3.09~Mb; Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}i and Supplementary Fig.~11d). Phased fractions differed by approximately 0.2 percentage points or less (Supplementary Fig.~11b).

Most of the switch-error difference arose from GNN correction. MethPhaser joined input blocks without changing phase within them. These joins increased switch errors by 2--15\% and Hamming distance by 4\% at 10$\times$ and 1.8--2.8-fold at 20--60$\times$. Before GNN correction, joint phasing and the SNV-only input to MethPhaser differed in switch-error count by at most 2\% (Supplementary Fig.~11). GNN correction accounted for 65--95\% of the switch-error difference between the final outputs, with MethPhaser's joins accounting for most of the remainder.

Without retraining the nanopore-derived network, \toolname had 2.4--2.8-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and 2.9--3.5-fold lower rates than \hapcut on HiFi data (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}j and Supplementary Table~11). Rates decreased from 0.049\% to 0.033\% for \toolname, from 0.128\% to 0.077\% for \whatshap and from 0.152\% to 0.096\% for \hapcut between 10 and 50$\times$. The difference from \whatshap narrowed from 2.6--2.8-fold at 10--30$\times$ to 2.4-fold at 40--50$\times$. Hamming distances were 0.7--1.2\% for all three tools; \toolname had the lowest values at 10--40$\times$ and the highest at 50$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}k). HiFi blocks were 2.5--4.3-fold shorter than nanopore blocks, consistent with the shorter reads.

\subsection*{Benchmark choice changes the measured separation between phasers}
Against GIAB v4.2.1, the three phasers converged as coverage increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}a). \toolname made 1,641--1,839 switch errors across coverages, whereas \whatshap and \hapcut decreased from 2,773 and 3,050 at 10$\times$ to 1,801 and 2,088 at 60$\times$. The ratio between the highest and lowest switch error rates fell from 1.6 to 1.2--1.3 at 30--60$\times$. Against v5.0q, errors decreased for all tools, including from 1,503 to 477 for \toolname, and the rate ratio increased from 3.3 to 4.3--5.0 (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}b). Relative to v4.2.1, v5.0q reduced errors for \toolname but increased them for \hapcut at every coverage and for \whatshap below 60$\times$. Hamming distance changed little (Supplementary Table~9).

Coincident switch errors accounted for much of the convergence under v4.2.1. The fraction of \toolname switch errors shared by both other tools increased from 63\% at 10$\times$ to 93\% at 60$\times$. At 60$\times$, these errors accounted for 86\% of \whatshap errors and 74\% of \hapcut errors; corresponding shares under v5.0q were only 38\%, 10\% and 8\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}c). In shared benchmark regions at 30--60$\times$, each tool made 1,133--1,492 switch errors against v4.2.1 but 32--393 against v5.0q (Supplementary Table~13). The truth sets thus differ in phase at many shared sites, although possible v4.2.1 phase-transfer errors have not been checked in the reads.

The separation between phasers also depended on genomic context. Within the v5.0q benchmark regions, \toolname had the lowest switch error rate of the three tools at every coverage. Rates were 2.1-fold lower than for \whatshap and 2.6-fold lower than for \hapcut at 10$\times$, increasing to 3.3--3.8-fold and 7.8--9.5-fold lower at 30--60$\times$ (Supplementary Table~13). Among GIAB genomic strata\cite{dwarshuis2024stratifications}, the separation was largest in segmental duplications (3.2--5.4-fold at 10 and 60$\times$) and smallest outside difficult regions (1.7--2.2-fold; Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}d,e). In regions newly covered by v5.0q\cite{olson2026giabv5}, \whatshap and \hapcut had 4.2--8.3 and 7.1--11 times the switch error rate of \toolname, compared with 1.7--2.5 and 2.0--9.4 times in shared benchmark regions. These comparisons involved 18--41 switch errors for \toolname in the newly covered regions per coverage.

Outside the benchmark regions of both truth sets, where the truth phase is least certain, switch errors accounted for an increasing share of each tool's total as coverage rose. This share increased from 28\% to 69\% for \toolname and from 43--46\% to 62--75\% for the other tools between 10 and 60$\times$. Rates in these regions were 0.8--1.1\% for \toolname and 2.6--4.9\% for the other tools. These sites contributed 50--56\% of the genome-wide difference in switch-error counts at 10$\times$ and 60--77\% at 60$\times$. We therefore treat this component of the comparison as exploratory (Supplementary Table~13).

\subsection*{\toolname phases slightly fewer benchmark SNVs and places fewer genotype errors on haplotypes than \whatshap}
\toolname phased slightly fewer benchmark heterozygous SNVs than \whatshap and \hapcut despite identical input calls. We measured completeness as the fraction of benchmark heterozygous SNVs phased. \whatshap and \hapcut phased 0.7--1.0 percentage points more than \toolname at every nanopore coverage. For all three tools, the phased fraction rose steeply up to 16$\times$ and plateaued at 91--92\% from 20$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}a and Supplementary Table~8). Most of the difference preceded GNN correction (0.6--0.9 percentage points); correction added 0.1--0.3. On HiFi data, \whatshap and \hapcut phased 0.4--0.5 percentage points more, and all three tools increased from 87\% at 10$\times$ to 92\% at 20--50$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}b). With indels, \whatshap phased 2.5--4.5 percentage points more benchmark heterozygous indels, with a larger difference at higher coverage (Supplementary Table~9).

A larger share of the calls phased by \toolname matched benchmark heterozygous SNVs. Among heterozygous SNV calls phased on chr1--22, the share matching a benchmark heterozygous SNV increased from 89.6\% to 93.3\% for \toolname and from 86.2\% to 91.5\% for \whatshap (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}c). The share was higher for \toolname in every replicate, although the difference narrowed from 3.4 percentage points at 10$\times$ to 1.8 at 50--60$\times$. F1, the harmonic mean of this share and completeness, was also higher in every replicate. The difference decreased from 0.9--1.1 percentage points at 10--18$\times$ to 0.4 at 50--60$\times$ (83.3--92.3\% for \toolname and 82.3--91.9\% for \whatshap; Supplementary Table~8).

\toolname also phased fewer genotype errors than \whatshap. These errors place an incorrect allele on a haplotype when a homozygous site is called and phased as heterozygous\cite{olson2026giabv5}. At positions with a homozygous v5.0q genotype on chr1--22, \whatshap phased 27,135, 13,706 and 11,815 heterozygous calls at 10, 30 and 60$\times$, compared with 14,154, 9,578 and 8,339 for \toolname (1.4--1.9 times as many; Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}d). \whatshap also phased 1.3--1.4 times as many calls with a different allele in v5.0q or no v5.0q record. The latter category included 190,344 calls for \whatshap and 147,307 for \toolname at 60$\times$; benchmark absence alone does not establish a genotype error.

Calls phased by only one tool explained these differences (Supplementary Table~17). \whatshap phased 124,000--151,000 calls that \toolname left unphased; 15--20\% matched benchmark heterozygous SNVs. Among benchmark-matching calls in blocks with at least two assessed variants, 22.8\%, 16.2\% and 9.9\% were phased incorrectly at 10, 30 and 60$\times$, three to six times its overall rate. Conversely, \toolname phased 34,000--50,000 calls that \whatshap left unphased. About 90\% were absent from the benchmark, and 92--93\% lay in segmental duplications or outside one-to-one assembly alignments. Among the 958--1,489 benchmark-matching SNVs assessed in such blocks, 12.7--14.9\% were phased incorrectly. Calls phased by either tool alone also overlapped that tool's switch-error intervals 2.4--9.9 times more often than its phased calls overall.

At higher sequencing coverage, calls left unphased by \toolname alone included more high-depth sites than those left unphased by \whatshap alone (Supplementary Fig.~12). At 10$\times$, 62--71\% of calls in each set had depth below 20$\times$. At 30 and 60$\times$, this remained true for 51--59\% of calls left unphased by \whatshap alone, compared with 12--17\% of those left unphased by \toolname alone. The latter set had a depth peak near the sequencing coverage and included more high-depth calls. At 60$\times$, 28\% had depth of at least 90$\times$, and 15.5\% reached the variant caller's depth cap. Only 3\% of calls left unphased by \whatshap alone reached 90$\times$.

The excess depth persisted when measured relative to sequencing coverage. At 30 and 60$\times$, 28\% and 17\% of calls left unphased by \toolname alone had at least twice the sequencing coverage, compared with 8\% and 2\% for \whatshap alone. Such depth can arise when reads from two paralogous copies map to one locus, a known source of false variant calls\cite{olson2026giabv5}. At 60$\times$, 3,669 calls left unphased by \toolname alone had depth of at least 90$\times$ within v5.0q benchmark regions; 92.8\% were absent from the benchmark VCF and thus classified as false positives by this benchmark. However, calls left unphased by \toolname alone overlapped segmental duplications or regions outside one-to-one assembly alignments less often than those left unphased by \whatshap alone (54--75\% versus 92--93\%). The source of the excess depth remains unresolved.

\subsection*{At 60$\times$, SNVs unphased by GNN correction lie mostly outside one-to-one assembly alignments}
Most SNVs unphased by GNN correction were absent by position from the v5.0q VCF. GNN correction unphased 61,389--64,201 SNVs per genome at 10--60$\times$ (one replicate per coverage). This step accounted for 17--20\% of the calls left unphased by \toolname alone at 10, 30 and 60$\times$; the rest were withheld before correction. Among GNN-unphased SNVs, the share absent by position from the v5.0q VCF increased from 84.0\% to 93.6\% with coverage, while the heterozygous share decreased from 10.9\% (6,684) to 3.5\% (2,156); the remainder were homozygous (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}e). Allele matching reduced the heterozygous counts to 6,409 and 1,999. Benchmark absence alone does not establish a call error because the benchmark depends on assembly alignment and variant representation.

An exploratory analysis of T2T-HG002 assembly alignments placed 11.4\% of the 62,230 SNVs unphased at 60$\times$ in regions where both haplotypes aligned one-to-one to GRCh38. This compared with 92.6\% of a random sample phased before correction (odds ratio for lying outside these regions, 98; Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}f and Supplementary Table~12). On autosomes alone, the shares were 9.1\% and 92.9\% (odds ratio, 131), with enrichment outside one-to-one alignments on every autosome (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}g). Sites outside the one-to-one alignment regions clustered in pericentromeric satellites, particularly at chr11:51--54~Mb, chr12:35--36~Mb and chr18:15--20~Mb. Of the 7,072 unphased SNVs within one-to-one alignments, 3,843 (54\%) had no assembly variant at the same start coordinate.

This assembly comparison identifies genomic context but does not independently validate phase or allele identity. Coordinate matching can miss variants represented at another position. The background sample included sites later unphased by correction, which biases the comparison towards no difference. The one-to-one regions came from raw assembly alignments and retain assembly errors removed during benchmark curation. Moreover, v5.0q derives from the same assembly. Thus, the analysis extends the assessment beyond benchmark regions, but cannot establish whether the excluded phase assignments were correct.

\subsection*{GNN correction reduces genome-wide SNV switch error rates by one-third and limits indel-associated errors}
To separate the contribution of GNN correction from initial phasing, we compared \toolname with and without this step. Before correction, SNV switch error rates were already 1.9--2.5-fold lower than for \whatshap and 2.2--3.3-fold lower than for \hapcut (Supplementary Fig.~13b and Supplementary Table~8). Correction reduced rates by a further 32--36\% at every coverage (31--38\% across individual replicates at 10--20$\times$). The associated loss of completeness decreased with coverage, from 0.26 percentage points at 10$\times$ to 0.08--0.11 at 30--60$\times$. Block N50 decreased by 5--9\% at 10--20$\times$ and 1--4\% at 30--60$\times$ (Supplementary Fig.~13a,d).

The correction effect was smaller on held-out chromosomes and HiFi data. On held-out chromosomes, correction removed 22--25\% of switch errors at 10--20$\times$ and 14--20\% at 30--60$\times$ (9--17 per single run). It withheld 9.3 phased SNVs per removed error, compared with 8.0 genome-wide (Supplementary Table~15). Every tool had higher switch error rates on these chromosomes than genome-wide. The smaller reduction may therefore reflect both chromosome difficulty and sequence absent from training. On HiFi data, correction removed 23--31\% of switch errors while reducing completeness by at most 0.12 percentage points and block N50 by 0.8--1.6\% (Supplementary Fig.~13e,f).
