\subsection*{\toolname combines joint phasing with learned error detection}
\toolname takes long-read alignments, a reference genome and heterozygous variant calls, and phases four variant classes together (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a). SNVs, small indels and SVs come from upstream variant callers (e.g., Clair3\cite{zheng2022clair3}, DeepVariant\cite{poplin2018deepvariant} and Sniffles2\cite{smolka2024sniffles2}). \toolname calls allele-specific 5mC sites from the same reads, keeping CpGs whose methylated and unmethylated reads are balanced and whose methylation co-segregates with a neighbouring heterozygous marker (Methods). Each heterozygous variant and 5mC site contributes a reference and an alternate allele node to one graph per chromosome, and each read links the alleles it carries (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). Methylation is therefore phased together with the sequence alleles of the same reads, instead of being used afterwards to join blocks whose sequence phase is already fixed. SVs inform phasing within blocks but never connect two blocks on their own.

Read evidence is weighted by its quality and its sequence context (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}b). Observations at low-quality bases and indels in tandem repeats are down-weighted, because both can link the wrong alleles. A copy-number-aware filter removes variants whose alternate-supporting reads carry excess heterozygous mismatches in intervals bounded by clipped alignments, which marks candidate copy-number-altered regions. Haplotypes are then resolved by multi-neighbour voting: each variant votes for an allele pairing with each downstream neighbour, and a pair without a clear majority casts no vote. A second, read-based pass assigns reads to haplotypes and leaves a variant unphased when the two read populations disagree on its phase.

\toolname then scores the uncertainty of every phase assignment and re-examines the least certain ones (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}c). Uncertainty is measured as phasing entropy, from 0 bits when all haplotype support agrees to 1 bit for an even split. Around each high-entropy variant, a graph neural network (GNN) reads the local phasing graph, combining message passing along its edges with self-attention across the window, and predicts where the phase starts to disagree with that of the neighbouring variants. Predicted variants are unphased rather than flipped (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}d), so an incorrect prediction cannot add a switch error.

\subsection*{\toolname has lower switch error rates than other phasers for SNVs and indels on both platforms}
We evaluated \toolname on HG002 nanopore R10.4.1 reads at 10--60$\times$ and PacBio HiFi reads at 10--50$\times$, with the same alignments and variant calls for every phaser (Methods). Phase was scored against two HG002 truth sets: GIAB v4.2.1, integrated from mapping-based call sets, and GIAB v5.0q, phased by the diploid telomere-to-telomere (T2T) assembly. Unless stated otherwise, results refer to SNV phasing against v5.0q.

In SNV-only phasing of nanopore data, \toolname had the lowest switch error rates at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}a and Supplementary Table~1): 0.022--0.081\%, compared with 0.081--0.233\% for \whatshap, a 2.9--3.9-fold difference. Its rates were 3.3--5.0-fold lower than those of \hapcut. \toolname also had 1.3--1.8-fold lower block-wise Hamming distances, the fraction of misassigned variants within blocks, than \whatshap and \hapcut (Supplementary Fig.~1a). When indels were included in the nanopore runs, \toolname had 3.0--3.7-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and 3.5--4.8-fold lower rates than \hapcut (Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}b). The difference was larger for indel pairs, adjacent variants of which at least one is an indel: \toolname had 8--32-fold lower indel-pair switch error rates than \whatshap (0.029--0.100\% versus 0.75--0.91\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:phasers}b and Supplementary Table~2). The fold difference increased with coverage because the rate of \toolname decreased and that of \whatshap did not. Indel Hamming distances showed the same pattern: \toolname had 1.8--2.7-fold lower values than \whatshap (3.5--7.1\% versus 9.0--13.8\%), and the fold difference increased with coverage (Supplementary Fig.~1b). Inside the v5.0q benchmark regions, the rates of all tools were lower and \toolname again had the lowest at every coverage: 2.1--3.8-fold lower SNV switch error rates than \whatshap and 2.6--9.5-fold lower rates than \hapcut in SNV-only phasing, and 9--56-fold lower indel-pair switch error rates than \whatshap (Supplementary Fig.~2).

\subsection*{Joint phasing extends lower error rates to SVs and 5mC}

We next asked whether the lower error rates of \toolname extend to SVs. Because SVs are sparse, we scored each heterozygous deletion and insertion of at least 50~bp called by Sniffles2 separately, not as adjacent variant pairs. An SV was wrongly phased when it was placed on a different haplotype from that in the T2T-HG002 assembly (Methods). On nanopore data, \toolname had 1.6--2.1-fold lower SV phasing error rates than \whatshap at every coverage (5.8--11.3\% versus 11.2--18.7\% at 10--60$\times$; Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}a and Supplementary Table~5). We found that \whatshap made two types of SV error more often than \toolname. It placed 1.8--2.3 times as many heterozygous SVs on the wrong haplotype and phased 2.7--3.3 times as many SVs that are homozygous in the assembly, which are genotype errors of the variant caller. On HiFi data, \toolname had 1.4--1.5-fold lower SV phasing error rates than \whatshap at every coverage (5.8--6.6\% versus 8.9--9.4\%; Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}b). HiPhase had SV phasing error rates similar to those of \toolname (5.4--6.4\% versus 5.8--6.6\%). With indels co-phased, \toolname had 1.7--2.3-fold lower SV phasing error rates than \whatshap on nanopore data and 1.3--1.7-fold lower rates on HiFi data, and rates similar to those of HiPhase (Supplementary Fig.~3). For SVs of at least 1~kb, \toolname had the lowest SV phasing error rates at every coverage on both platforms (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}c): 8.0--12.8\% on nanopore data, compared with 15.2--20.7\% for \whatshap, and 7.8--9.1\% on HiFi data, compared with 10.9--13.3\% for HiPhase and 13.1--14.3\% for \whatshap. For SVs of 50~bp to 1~kb on HiFi data, HiPhase had the lowest SV phasing error rates (4.8--5.8\%, compared with 5.6--6.5\% for \toolname; Supplementary Table~5).

We then compared the joint SNV and 5mC phasing of \toolname with MethPhaser\cite{fu2024methphaser}, which adds methylation after phasing: given the SNV-only phasing of \toolname from the same reads and SNV calls (Methods), it joins the blocks of that phasing without changing phase within them. On nanopore data, \toolname had 1.5--1.8-fold lower switch error rates than MethPhaser at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}d and Supplementary Table~4). MethPhaser produced longer blocks than \toolname at every coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:methylation}e and Supplementary Table~4). At 10--60$\times$, MethPhaser had N50 1.1--1.8-fold that of \toolname (0.96--5.49 versus 0.87--3.12~Mb), at phased fractions within 0.2 percentage points. MethPhaser lengthens blocks by joining them with methylation evidence. Because one wrong join places a whole block on the opposite haplotype, Hamming distance measures this error better than the switch error rate, which counts it as a single switch (Supplementary Fig.~1e). In MethPhaser blocks, 4.0--7.2\% of SNVs were on the wrong haplotype, compared with 1.7--5.3\% for \toolname and 1.8--6.9\% in the input of MethPhaser before its joins (Supplementary Fig.~4). \subsection*{GIAB v5.0q reveals larger differences in switch error rates among phasing tools}
The same phased outputs separated the tools more clearly against v5.0q than against v4.2.1. Against GIAB v4.2.1, differences in switch error rates among \toolname, \whatshap and \hapcut narrowed as coverage increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}a). At 10--60$\times$ coverage, \toolname made 1,641--1,839 switch errors, compared with 1,801--2,773 for \whatshap and 2,088--3,050 for \hapcut. Against v5.0q at the same coverages, \toolname made 477--1,503 switch errors, compared with 1,785--4,383 for \whatshap and 2,309--5,018 for \hapcut (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}b). For all three tools, block-wise Hamming distances were similar between the two benchmarks across coverages (Supplementary Table~7). Under v4.2.1, many switch errors occurred at the same positions in all three tools (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}c). At 60$\times$ coverage, these shared positions contained 93\% of \toolname switch errors, 86\% of \whatshap switch errors and 74\% of \hapcut switch errors. When the same outputs were assessed against v5.0q, the fractions shared by all three tools were substantially lower: 38\% for \toolname, 10\% for \whatshap and 8\% for \hapcut, indicating less overlap in switch-error positions across tools than under v4.2.1. Differences between the benchmarks were also evident within genomic regions covered by both. For the same 60$\times$ outputs, \toolname, \whatshap and \hapcut had 1,180, 1,245 and 1,435 switch errors against v4.2.1, respectively, compared with 32, 63 and 273 against v5.0q (Supplementary Table~8). These results suggest that the two benchmarks assign different phases at many shared sites. The underlying reads have not been examined to determine which phase assignments are correct.

We compared the three tools across six regional categories: all v5.0q benchmark regions, regions outside GIAB difficult-region annotations\cite{dwarshuis2024stratifications}, segmental duplications, regions shared by v4.2.1 and v5.0q, regions newly covered by v5.0q\cite{olson2026giabv5}, and regions outside both benchmarks (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmarks}d,e). Within the v5.0q benchmark regions, the difference in switch error rates between \toolname and the other tools was larger in segmental duplications than outside difficult regions. In regions covered by v5.0q but not v4.2.1, \toolname had 20 switch errors at 60$\times$ coverage, compared with 88 for \whatshap and 148 for \hapcut. At the same coverage, regions outside both benchmarks accounted for 77\% of the genome-wide difference in switch-error counts between \whatshap and \toolname, and 60\% of the difference between \hapcut and \toolname. Because the reference phase is less certain outside the benchmark regions, we treat this last comparison as exploratory (Supplementary Table~8).

\subsection*{\toolname leaves unphased the SNV calls that rarely match the benchmark}
The heterozygous SNV calls that \toolname left unphased rarely matched the benchmark. At 10, 30 and 60$\times$ nanopore coverage, 89.9--93.3\% of the calls that \toolname phased matched v5.0q heterozygous SNVs in position and alleles, compared with only 9.8--13.0\% of the calls that it left unphased (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}a). Of the unphased calls, 3.3--7.6\% were homozygous in v5.0q and are therefore genotype errors, whereas 81--86\% were absent from the benchmark. These calls are false positives within its assessed regions and remain unclassified elsewhere because of alignment or structural complexity\cite{olson2026giabv5}. \toolname left 202,000--236,000 calls unphased, compared with 124,000--141,000 for \whatshap. Calls absent from the benchmark or classified as homozygous accounted for 66--79\% of this difference (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}b). The homozygous category identifies sites incorrectly called heterozygous, which introduce incorrect alleles when phased. \toolname phased 29--48\% fewer of these genotype errors than \whatshap, including 8,339 versus 11,815 at 60$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}c). \whatshap incorrectly phased 9.9--22.8\% of the assessable benchmark SNVs that \toolname left unphased, compared with 2.6--7.7\% across its phased benchmark SNVs overall (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}d and Supplementary Table~9).

Precision, the fraction of phased SNV calls matching the benchmark, was consistently higher for \toolname than for \whatshap across nanopore coverages (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}e). The cost was a small decrease in recall, the fraction of benchmark heterozygous SNVs matched and phased, which was 0.7--1.0 percentage points lower for \toolname than for \whatshap and \hapcut across coverages; all three tools reached 91--92\% from 20$\times$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}f). F1, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was higher for \toolname than for \whatshap in every replicate (Supplementary Table~1). The same pattern held on HiFi data. Across 10--50$\times$ coverage, precision was 92.2--95.0\% for \toolname, compared with 90.3--94.6\% for \whatshap and 91.1--93.9\% for HiPhase. \toolname had the highest precision at every coverage, with recall 0.2--0.5 percentage points lower than that of the other two tools (Supplementary Fig.~6a,b and Supplementary Table~3). Larger differences in the fraction phased were observed for indels and SVs (Supplementary Tables~4, 5 and~7).

Some calls left unphased by \toolname had excess read depth or lay outside one-to-one assembly alignments. At 60$\times$ coverage, 28\% of calls phased only by \whatshap had read depth $\geq$90$\times$, compared with 3\% of calls phased only by \toolname (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}g and Supplementary Fig.~7). Within v5.0q benchmark regions, 92.8\% of the 3,669 high-depth calls phased only by \whatshap had no corresponding benchmark variant. Excess depth may reflect additional sequence copies in HG002 relative to the reference\cite{olson2026giabv5}. In an exploratory analysis of a subset of unphased SNVs at 60$\times$, 88.6\% lay outside regions where both haplotypes of the T2T-HG002 assembly aligned one-to-one to GRCh38, compared with 7.4\% of a random sample of initially phased SNVs (Fig.~\ref{fig:unphased}h and Supplementary Table~10). Such regions can be difficult to assess because of structural or copy-number differences between HG002 and GRCh38\cite{olson2026giabv5}. The alignment result alone does not establish call or phasing errors. Overall, \toolname phased fewer genotype errors than \whatshap, with only a small reduction in recall. On one thread, \toolname also phased SNVs 9.6- to 34.2-fold faster than \whatshap and \hapcut with the lowest peak memory of the three tools. With 24 threads, it phased a 60$\times$ nanopore genome in less than one minute (Supplementary Fig.~8 and Supplementary Table~11).
